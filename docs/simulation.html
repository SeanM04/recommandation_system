Simulated world · Phase 1

Customers, offers and the behaviour the recommender must discover

The recommender is tested against a world whose true behaviour we control. This page documents that world: what can be offered, who the customers are, how likely each one is to take each offer, and how they react to a ranked list.

Everything below the offer catalogue and customer profiles is hidden from the recommender. It is shown here for reviewers, and is used only by the simulator and the offline regret calculation.

How a take-up probability is set

Hidden

Every segment has a base rate per offer. Named context effects then nudge it up or down, and the result is capped. One readable table plus a short list of effects describes the whole behaviour model.

Worked example

A family customer on contract, mid-range handset, R420 a month, opening the app on a Saturday evening in payday week, shown funeral cover:

  • Revenue changes the answer. Optimising margin × take-up moves students to 5GB, young professionals and small businesses to 20GB, and pensioners to funeral cover. The reward function is a business choice, not a detail.
  • Payday changes the answer for two segments. Pensioners buy airtime for most of the month and electricity in SASSA grant week. Small businesses want pay-later in the mid-month cash squeeze and 20GB data in payday week. A policy with segment × payday statistics can learn both; a per-segment policy can only learn the average.

Behaviour shift

Hidden

Halfway through a simulation the world changes in two ways at once. A streaming bundle goes viral with young professionals and partly cannibalises their 5GB data bundle: a rarely shown offer becomes a hit. An electricity tariff hike makes the cashback offer families' first choice: a well-known runner-up overtakes the favourite. The second kind is the one that needs forgetting, because the model is confident about the old rates (decision P9).

How customers respond to a list

Hidden

The API returns several offers in order. The customer scans from the top, may not notice lower positions, and stops after taking one up. This gives the three outcomes the API accepts a precise meaning, and it makes ranking order matter.

Position in the list#1#2#3#4#5
Chance the customer notices it100%60%40%25%15%

Because the model is this simple, the expected reward of any ranked list can be computed exactly: each position adds P(nothing taken above it) × attention × p × value. The regret oracle in Phase 5 uses that formula with the true probabilities.

Consequence for learning. An ignored offer was usually never seen, so it says little about how appealing the offer is. Policies learn from taken_up and viewed and skip ignored unless learn_from_ignored is set; see the reward functions.
