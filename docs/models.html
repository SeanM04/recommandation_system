Models

Every model, one by one

Nine policies decide which three offers each customer sees. They are built from four ideas: learning online, personalising by segment, exploring by doubt, and forgetting. Each one adds a single idea to another, so comparing them shows what each idea is worth. Behind them sit a handful of supporting models: the belief each policy keeps, the reward it learns from, and the simulated customers it is tested on.

How the policies relate

Read top to bottom: every arrow adds one idea. Each box shows how much of what was possible the policy achieved in the published run: the take-ups an oracle that knows every customer's true preferences would have won.

How each kind decides and learns

There are four kinds of model. The variants of each differ only in configuration, so the same flow applies to all of them.

The baselines: random offers and the fixed popularity ranking

Epsilon-greedy: exploit the best average, explore a fixed share of the time

Thompson sampling: explore in proportion to doubt

The Policies page shows the code of each, copied from the source when the docs are built.

The nine policies

Each card says what the model is for here and where you will meet it, then its configuration and how it did on the same 50,000 app opens as every other policy.

Random offers random

The idea
Shows the eligible offers in a random order.
How it ranks
Draws a random number for each offer the customer is eligible for and shows the three highest.
How it learns
Nothing.
Settings
none
Its role here
The floor. A policy that can't beat random offers isn't learning anything.
Where it's used
The comparison; the bottom bar of the comparison page's ladder. Any request can ask for it with ?policy=random.
Results
149 take-ups per 1,000 app opens · 52.6% of what was possible · regret 6,728 ± 17 · R5.80 revenue per app open · #9 of 9

Fixed popularity ranking static

The idea
Last month's most popular offers, the same for everyone, frozen until the next retrain.
How it ranks
Scores each eligible offer by its average reward in a historical log and shows the top three; ties keep catalogue order.
How it learns
Never online. It is fitted once: on a 5,000-app-open warm-up month in the comparison, and on the event log when the API starts.
Settings
fitted from history; no settings
Its role here
The usual batch approach, and the bar the brief asks to beat. Called the fixed popularity ranking in the dashboard.
Where it's used
The comparison; the baseline in every dashboard story. Any request can ask for it with ?policy=static.
Results
172 take-ups per 1,000 app opens · 60.8% of what was possible · regret 5,563 ± 703 · R4.42 revenue per app open · #8 of 9

Epsilon-greedy, one model epsilon_greedy

The idea
Mostly the offers with the best average so far; now and then a random one.
How it ranks
For each of the three slots: with probability ε pick a random remaining offer (explore), otherwise the best remaining average (exploit). An offer never shown scores 1.0, so every offer is tried early.
How it learns
Counts responses and sums rewards per offer, for all customers together.
Settings
beliefs everyone together; ε = 0.1; reward: 1 per take-up
Its role here
The classic simple bandit with one view of everyone: learning without personalisation or smart exploration.
Where it's used
The comparison. Any request can ask for it with ?policy=epsilon_greedy.
Results
186 take-ups per 1,000 app opens · 65.9% of what was possible · regret 4,836 ± 359 · R4.97 revenue per app open · #7 of 9

Epsilon-greedy per segment epsilon_greedy_segment

The idea
Epsilon-greedy, with a separate average per customer segment.
How it ranks
As epsilon-greedy, using the customer's segment's averages.
How it learns
Counts and sums per offer and segment.
Settings
beliefs per segment; ε = 0.1; reward: 1 per take-up
Its role here
Personalised but exploring blindly: the comparison point for Thompson sampling with the same segments.
Where it's used
The comparison; the Explore vs exploit page (it never stops exploring). Any request can ask for it with ?policy=epsilon_greedy_segment.
Results
250 take-ups per 1,000 app opens · 88.1% of what was possible · regret 1,690 ± 381 · R9.94 revenue per app open · #4 of 9

Thompson sampling, one model thompson

The idea
Keeps a belief about each offer's take-up rate and shows the offers whose beliefs drew highest.
How it ranks
Draws one plausible take-up rate from each offer's Beta belief and shows the three highest draws. Offers it is unsure about sometimes draw high and get tested: that is all the exploration there is.
How it learns
A take-up adds 1 to α, a view adds 1 to β; one belief per offer for all customers.
Settings
beliefs everyone together; reward: 1 per take-up
Its role here
Thompson sampling without personalisation: isolates what segments add.
Where it's used
The comparison; the comparison page's ladder (one model for everyone). Any request can ask for it with ?policy=thompson.
Results
193 take-ups per 1,000 app opens · 67.7% of what was possible · regret 4,582 ± 274 · R5.09 revenue per app open · #6 of 9

Thompson sampling per segment thompson_segment

The idea
Thompson sampling with one belief per offer and customer segment.
How it ranks
As Thompson sampling, drawing from the customer's segment's beliefs.
How it learns
Per offer and segment. Evidence only accumulates.
Settings
beliefs per segment; reward: 1 per take-up
Its role here
Personalisation on its own, and the policy without forgetting that the stories compare forgetting against.
Where it's used
The comparison; the Explore vs exploit and Behaviour shift pages. Any request can ask for it with ?policy=thompson_segment.
Results
261 take-ups per 1,000 app opens · 92.4% of what was possible · regret 1,078 ± 166 · R10.01 revenue per app open · #2 of 9

Thompson per segment and payday week thompson_segment_payday

The idea
Thompson sampling per segment and payday week.
How it ranks
Draws from the beliefs for the customer's segment in this part of the month: payday week or the rest.
How it learns
Two beliefs per offer and segment, one for payday week and one for the rest of the month.
Settings
beliefs per segment and payday week; reward: 1 per take-up
Its role here
Tests whether payday context helps: pensioners and small businesses want different offers at payday. It splits the evidence in two to find out.
Where it's used
The comparison. Any request can ask for it with ?policy=thompson_segment_payday.
Results
261 take-ups per 1,000 app opens · 92.3% of what was possible · regret 1,099 ± 127 · R10.16 revenue per app open · #3 of 9

Thompson per segment, with forgetting thompson_segment_decay Serving

The idea
Thompson sampling per segment that slowly forgets old evidence.
How it ranks
As Thompson sampling per segment.
How it learns
Before each update, every offer's evidence in the segment shrinks by γ, then the new response is added, so old behaviour fades.
Settings
beliefs per segment; forgetting γ = 0.9998 (memory ≈ 5,000 responses); reward: 1 per take-up
Its role here
The policy the API serves. Forgetting costs a little in quiet times and recovers much faster when customers change.
Where it's used
Serving every app open; the learner in every dashboard story; Try it's default. Any request can ask for it with ?policy=thompson_segment_decay.
Results
263 take-ups per 1,000 app opens · 92.7% of what was possible · regret 1,041 ± 39 · R9.68 revenue per app open · #1 of 9

Thompson with forgetting, from revenue thompson_segment_revenue

The idea
The served policy, learning from margin instead of take-ups.
How it ranks
As Thompson sampling per segment with forgetting.
How it learns
A take-up teaches the offer's margin divided by the top margin instead of 1, so high-margin offers count for more.
Settings
beliefs per segment; forgetting γ = 0.9998 (memory ≈ 5,000 responses); reward: margin ÷ top margin
Its role here
Shows that what a policy learns from is a business choice: more revenue for fewer take-ups.
Where it's used
The comparison; the comparison page's next steps. Any request can ask for it with ?policy=thompson_segment_revenue.
Results
205 take-ups per 1,000 app opens · 72.4% of what was possible · regret 3,916 ± 92 · R13.37 revenue per app open · #5 of 9

Settings from config/settings.yaml; results from the published run (10 seeds × 50,000 app opens) · rebuilt by make docs

The models behind the models

ModelWhat it doesUsed byCode
Beta beliefWhat a policy believes about one offer's take-up rate in one context. It starts at Beta(1, 1), "anything from 0 to 100% is equally likely"; then α = 1 + rewards and β = 1 + responses − rewards. Its mean, α ÷ (α + β), is the expected take-up rate, and its width is the doubt.Thompson sampling; the options store keeps responses and rewards, and the database computes α and β from them.store/options_store.py, store/sql.py
ForgettingBefore each update, all the evidence in the context is multiplied by γ, then the new response is added. Evidence levels off at about 1 ÷ (1 − γ) responses, so old behaviour fades.The two policies with forgetting (γ = 0.9998, about 5,000 responses).algorithms/base.py, store/
Context keyWhich belief a response updates and a ranking reads: one for everyone, one per segment, or one per segment and part of the month.Every learning policy.algorithms/base.py
RewardsTurn one outcome into what a policy learns: 1 for a take-up and 0 for a view (binary), or the offer's margin ÷ the top margin for a take-up (revenue). An ignored offer teaches nothing by default.Set per policy in settings.yaml; see the reward table.algorithms/rewards.py
Ground truthThe hidden probability that a given customer takes up a given offer at a given moment: a base rate per segment and offer, adjusted by 14 context effects (payday week, time of day, device, contract, spend and more), capped at 90%. It changes at the behaviour shift.The simulator only. No policy ever sees it.simulator/ground_truth.py; see Simulated world
Response modelHow a customer reads a list of offers: they notice the offer in position 1, 2, 3 … with probability 1.0, 0.6, 0.4, 0.25, 0.15, and take up at most one per app open.The simulator; the reason ignored offers teach nothing.simulator/ground_truth.py
OracleShows the three offers with the highest true probability. Regret is the oracle's expected take-ups minus the policy's: take-ups lost to not knowing.The comparison and every results page.simulator/engine.py
Story measuresChance of being best (drawn from the Beta beliefs), exploration share, evidence, time to switch (the new favourite leads 80% of the segment's last 100 app opens), and every claim counted seed by seed.The dashboard's Algorithm comparison, Explore vs exploit and Behaviour shift pages.evaluation/story.py, exploration.py, adaptation.py, metrics.py

How they are used here

  1. Serving. Every app open is ranked by the policy named in recommendation.active_policy in settings.yaml. Any request can ask for another with ?policy=, which is how an A/B test or a holdout would run. Each policy keeps its own beliefs in the options store, so all nine can learn side by side.
  2. Learning. POST /response looks up which policy served the app open, turns the outcome into that policy's reward, and updates its belief in one database transaction.
  3. Comparing. The simulator runs all nine on the identical 50,000 app opens, 10 times with different seeds, against the oracle. The Results page and the dashboard read what it saves.
  4. Explaining. The dashboard's stories cast four of them: the serving policy as the learner, the fixed popularity ranking as the baseline, Thompson per segment as the learner without forgetting, and epsilon-greedy per segment as the explorer that never stops.
